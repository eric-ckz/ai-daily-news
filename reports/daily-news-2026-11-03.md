   - 摘要：# When AI agents agree on the data but not what it means Presented by SAP Enterprise AI agents that share the same data can still make conflicting decisions because raw data rarely captures the business context — the processes, policies and priorities that determine what that data means to the company. As agents take on more decision-making, those gaps in context become harder to contain. Three problems emerge when multiple agents act without shared business semantics: identical figures lead different functions to different conclusions, agents that work correctly on their own combine into the wrong outcome, and accuracy degrades as data accumulates. Enterprises have spent years loading ever more records into central data platforms, separating them in the process from the policies, processes and business context where they originated. That separation is at the root of the problem, says Irfan Khan, president and chief product officer for SAP Data & Analytics. “If you access data from a source environment and it doesn’t have the context, the likelihood is you’re going to make an ill-informed decision,” he explains. "Users learn from history only when institutional knowledge helps them separate signal from noise. An agent starts with no such knowledge unless the company supplies it." ## The agent coordination gap in multi-agent AI systems Traditional data strategies extract records from source systems and centralize them, which separates the data from the semantics and process context it carried at the source. The shift toward separating storage and compute let companies scale each independently and encouraged many to build multiple data lakes alongside on-premises estates, flat files and object stores, leading to fragmented data increasingly separated from its original business context. In multi-agent systems, missing context doesn’t stay a data problem. Once agents divide a business process among them, it becomes a coordination failure. For instance, a procurement agent negotiates longer payment terms to improve margins, a treasury agent delays payments to preserve liquidity, and a compliance agent holds payments until every validation clears. Each one is performing its assigned job correctly, but together they're stalling supplier payments long enough that critical vendors start deprioritizing the company, and no internal system flags anything as broken. Plus, few business processes run on a single agent from end to end. An order entry agent might hand off to one that checks inventory, which hands off to another that files a procurement request or decrements stock. If those steps run out of sequence, one agent can decrement an account before another has confirmed the goods exist, and the task can be completed, but the outcome can still be wrong. And there's no reason agents would coordinate any better than people do when nobody manages the handoffs, Khan says. “It’s almost like pushing out a task to 20 individual people to go and get it done for a client, and then you realize that nobody held a team meeting,” he says. “Nobody kept an inventory of where you were, or where you are within the process.” ## A business data fabric gives agents shared business context Giving agents access to distributed enterprise data solves only part of the problem. They also need a common understanding of how the business expects that data to be interpreted and acted upon. SAP’s business data fabric architecture is designed to preserve that meaning as data moves across systems rather than separating it from its original business context. It connects data and its semantics with the processes, policies, and other business knowledge that govern how the data is used, while allowing agents to reach information across distributed environments. Every answer, inference, agent, and AI workflow carries a real infrastructure cost, and that cost isn't determined by the model alone. It rises or falls with compute efficiency, utilization, power, cooling, networking, latency, and workload architecture. The same intelligence can have radically different economics depending on where, when, and how it is delivered. Back in March 2023, running a GPT-4-class model cost roughly $20 per million tokens. Yet the average enterprise AI budget has grown nearly six times over the same period, from $1.2 million in 2024 to approximately $7 million in 2026. Intelligence has never been cheaper. AI has never cost more. Unit price is falling while total spend rises. That is the paradox defining AI infrastructure in 2026, and it is why the industry’s most important conversation is shifting from how much capacity we can build to how much usable intelligence that capacity can deliver, per dollar, per watt, and per second. What follows is the case for that shift: the economics behind it, the chips accelerating it, and the power constraint quietly capping it. ## From capacity to productivity The first phase of the AI infrastructure race was necessarily about capacity. Compute was scarce, demand was accelerating, and hyperscalers, enterprises, and governments moved quickly to secure GPUs, power, and data center capacity. That capacity remains essential, but it doesn't by itself determine AI economics. A GPU cluster running at high utilization has very different unit economics from the same cluster sitting partially idle. The question is evolving from how much compute is available to how productively that compute converts into usable intelligence.
