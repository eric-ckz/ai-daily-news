Announced on 2 November 2026, the service uses facial verification and cryptographic authentication to confirm that an authorized person has approved a specific action before an AI agent can execute it. The technology implements the Human Approval and Presence Specification (HAPS), introduced in September 2026, and integrates with open-source AI infrastructure platform agentgateway and authorization management provider Cerbos. Under the system, an AI agent attempting a high-risk transaction, such as transferring funds, can be required to obtain human authorization. But some actions must remain subject to human control, approval and accountability,” said iProov founder and CEO Andrew Bud. Cerbos will determine when human approval is required, while iProov verifies the approver’s identity and presence. The launch comes as businesses increasingly deploy AI agents capable of independently executing tasks, raising concerns about unauthorized transactions, accountability and security. The HAPS specification is open source, allowing other technology providers to develop compatible approval systems.    - 摘要：Anthropic is working on an open-source system to verify which AI model produced a given output, according to a blog post published Tuesday. The system, called FlashProve, is built to provide what researchers call “provable inference” – a cryptographic proof, essentially a mathematical guarantee, that a given output came from a specific model. It’s meant to let users confirm that the answer they received really came from the model they think they’re using. Similar to cryptographic email signatures that let recipients confirm a message really came from its claimed sender, FlashProve would be robust against impostors attempting to disguise one model as another, even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model. In particular, the researchers note that the system could be used to prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive model to inflate its performance results. FlashProve could also be used to verify claims by third parties that a specific task was done by an advanced model. “A developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model,” the researchers write. “How can a user verify these claims?” To work, the system requires AI labs to publish a “fingerprint” of each model they release that can later be matched to specific outputs and verified cryptographically. Crucially, the fingerprints don’t reveal a model’s weights, the internal numbers that define how it behaves and which closed-model makers like Anthropic and OpenAI keep private. That means labs could publish the fingerprints without giving up their most valuable asset, enabling the system to work with commercial models, too. The system is also designed to be lightweight, so verification could be performed on a local device.
