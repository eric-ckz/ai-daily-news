The numbers carry a specific implication that the headline figure alone does not capture. OpenAI's record $122 billion March fundraising round — the largest private capital raise in Silicon Valley history at the time — is projected to be exhausted by approximately 2028 at the company's planned spending pace. That is two years before OpenAI's own revenue trajectory is projected to reach its peak, and two years before the company's internal models predict it will achieve positive cash flow. Bloomberg reported on September 15 that investors had approached OpenAI about a new private round that would value the company at more than $1.2 trillion — a roughly 41% premium over its $852 billion March valuation. Forbes separately reported that OpenAI is itself seeking a valuation as high as $1.5 trillion in those discussions. The talks are investor-initiated, Bloomberg's source said — a detail that matters because it signals ongoing institutional demand for OpenAI exposure despite the company's structural cash deficit. The $278 billion deficit is not an accounting artifact or a strategic choice to prioritize growth over profitability. It reflects a structural feature of how large language models are deployed at scale. Training an AI model is a one-time cost. OpenAI spends months and tens of billions of dollars teaching a model by processing vast datasets across GPU clusters — an expense that, large as it is, ends when training is complete. The persistent cost is inference: every time a user sends a message to ChatGPT or calls the API, OpenAI's infrastructure must run that specific query through its models on GPU hardware, consuming electricity and compute time to generate the response, as documented in NBER working paper research on AI firm economics. ChatGPT now serves more than 900 million weekly users and processes more than 2.5 billion daily prompts globally, per OpenAI's March 2026 fundraising announcement. High-performance H100 GPU capacity on cloud platforms runs approximately $7 per GPU-hour on demand; sustaining 900 million weekly users across billions of daily queries requires thousands of GPU-hours per minute, continuously. Research firm Sacra estimated OpenAI's inference costs reached $8.4 billion in 2025 and projected they would rise to $14.1 billion in 2026 — a 68% increase — driven not by inefficiency but by user growth and the greater compute demands of more capable reasoning models. The AI Energy Management Alliance is designed to steer the surging power appetite of AI data centers more precisely and to plug those facilities into existing grids as flexible loads rather than rigid, round-the-clock drains. The initiative answers a bottleneck that CEO Jensen Huang had already flagged. According to Reuters, the company said the buildout would more than double the entire continent's current computing load. The message is blunt: anyone who wants to run high-performance AI eventually collides with territorial and energy limits. 7. **OpenAI Projects $278B Cash Burn: Record Round Runs Dry Before Revenue Catches Up**
   - 摘要：An internal OpenAI presentation prepared in July for a computing deal projects $278 billion in negative free cash flow from 2026 through the end of 2030 — a figure the Financial Times first reported Thursday and Bloomberg confirmed the same day. The company expects to spend roughly $856 billion on computing infrastructure through 2030, while generating about $840 billion in cumulative revenue over the same period. The numbers carry a specific implication that the headline figure alone does not capture. OpenAI's record $122 billion March fundraising round — the largest private capital raise in Silicon Valley history at the time — is projected to be exhausted by approximately 2028 at the company's planned spending pace. That is two years before OpenAI's own revenue trajectory is projected to reach its peak, and two years before the company's internal models predict it will achieve positive cash flow. The company is essentially confirming, through its own July document, that the $122 billion was not runway to self-sufficiency — it was runway to the next fundraise. That fundraise appears to have already begun. Bloomberg reported on September 15 that investors had approached OpenAI about a new private round that would value the company at more than $1.2 trillion — a roughly 41% premium over its $852 billion March valuation. Forbes separately reported that OpenAI is itself seeking a valuation as high as $1.5 trillion in those discussions. The talks are investor-initiated, Bloomberg's source said — a detail that matters because it signals ongoing institutional demand for OpenAI exposure despite the company's structural cash deficit. The $278 billion deficit is not an accounting artifact or a strategic choice to prioritize growth over profitability. It reflects a structural feature of how large language models are deployed at scale. Training an AI model is a one-time cost. OpenAI spends months and tens of billions of dollars teaching a model by processing vast datasets across GPU clusters — an expense that, large as it is, ends when training is complete. The persistent cost is inference: every time a user sends a message to ChatGPT or calls the API, OpenAI's infrastructure must run that specific query through its models on GPU hardware, consuming electricity and compute time to generate the response, as documented in NBER working paper research on AI firm economics. ChatGPT now serves more than 900 million weekly users and processes more than 2.5 billion daily prompts globally, per OpenAI's March 2026 fundraising announcement. Serving that volume requires enormous sustained GPU capacity — not a one-time purchase but an ongoing lease, because the compute required to answer each query is consumed and must be immediately available for the next one. High-performance H100 GPU capacity on cloud platforms runs approximately $7 per GPU-hour on demand; sustaining 900 million weekly users across billions of daily queries requires thousands of GPU-hours per minute, continuously. Research firm Sacra estimated OpenAI's inference costs reached $8.4 billion in 2025 and projected they would rise to $14.1 billion in 2026 — a 68% increase — driven not by inefficiency but by user growth and the greater compute demands of more capable reasoning models. This is why $856 billion in infrastructure spending is projected through 2030 even as revenue is expected to reach $350 billion by that year. The gap does not mean the business is failing to scale — OpenAI's own projections show revenue climbing from approximately $36 billion in 2026 to that $350 billion figure in 2030, a roughly tenfold increase in four years, confirmed by Bloomberg. It means the cost to serve users at frontier-model quality is currently growing faster than the price customers pay per token — and the difference is being funded by investor capital.
