As artificial intelligence systems become increasingly capable of working on their own, keeping them focused on their assigned tasks is a growing challenge. University of Florida computer science professor Sumit Jha is working to keep AI agents from “going rogue.” Jha and his team have developed a system that can detect when an AI model begins to drift from its assigned task and steer it back without retraining the model or significantly increasing computing demands. The research comes as news reports have chronicled AI agents gaining greater autonomy and occasionally behaving in ways their developers did not intend. Recent incidents have included AI agents accessing government websites and an Anthropic model submitting an anonymous tip to a police department in July, claiming to have information about an unsolved murder. Jha, a professor in UF’s Department of Computer & Information Science & Engineering, or [CISE](https://cise.ufl.edu/), studies how to make AI systems more controllable and understandable. He is working to engineer simple, noninvasive ways to keep systems on task. Jha and his collaborators have authored a paper titled “Drift No More: Spectral Locking for Long-Horizon Open-Weight Multi-agent AI,” detailing a system dubbed ATLAS, which stands for Algebraic Task-role Locking with Adaptive Spectral Memory. The acronym hints at the dense mathematics at the heart of the system. Jha and his co-authors, Vishal Pramanik, Maisha Maliha and Susmit Jha, devised a system that works inside the large language model, or LLM, as it generates responses. When the model begins to drift from its task, ATLAS steers the LLM back toward its assignment, its original responsibility and the latest shared information. “An AI agent can start going wrong before its response looks wrong,” Jha explained. “Inside the model, it may already be drifting from the task, confusing its role or relying on information that has been replaced. ATLAS is built to correct that drift before it reaches the next message, so other agents do not build on the mistake.” Existing strategies usually involve policing an AI agent’s answers after the fact. Agent Compute Engine gives each AI agent its own hardware-isolated microVM while keeping credentials outside the sandbox where the agent runs. Google Cloud says this is intended to combine the separation associated with virtual machines with the speed and density more commonly linked to containers. The service is used across four broad categories inside Google: consumer and enterprise agent tools, software engineering workspaces, large-scale model evaluation and reinforcement learning, and sandbox environments for internal services that need to run dynamic logic. Google's description of the product reflects a wider challenge facing cloud providers and AI companies as they move from chatbots to software agents. According to Google Cloud, traditional virtual machines offer isolation but can take seconds or minutes to start, suspend or resume. Containers are faster, but may not provide the same level of separation for workloads that require deeper control of the environment. AI agents also often spend much of their time waiting between model steps or user instructions, making permanently active virtual machines expensive and inefficient. **Security model** A central part of the system is a design that assumes code inside the sandbox may be untrusted. The microVMs hold no persistent secrets, OAuth refresh tokens or cloud service account keys, and they do not have access to a cloud instance metadata server. Instead, outbound requests are routed through local and external proxies, with identities and credentials managed off the node. When an agent requests access to a document or private resource, the request is checked outside the sandbox before the appropriate credential is applied. The platform also uses Google's Titan and Titanium infrastructure components as part of its trust model. Private keys for node and workload attestation are kept outside the guest environment, while storage attachments are mediated by the infrastructure processing unit. In-context learning lets a person show a model three examples of a product description written in a house style and get a fourth back, in seconds, for the price of a few hundred extra [tokens](https://ppc.land/tokens/).
