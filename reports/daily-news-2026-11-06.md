As artificial intelligence systems become increasingly capable of working on their own, keeping them focused on their assigned tasks is a growing challenge. University of Florida computer science professor Sumit Jha is working to keep AI agents from “going rogue.” Jha and his team have developed a system that can detect when an AI model begins to drift from its assigned task and steer it back without retraining the model or significantly increasing computing demands. The research comes as news reports have chronicled AI agents gaining greater autonomy and occasionally behaving in ways their developers did not intend. Recent incidents have included AI agents accessing government websites and an Anthropic model submitting an anonymous tip to a police department in July, claiming to have information about an unsolved murder. Jha, a professor in UF’s Department of Computer & Information Science & Engineering, or [CISE](https://cise.ufl.edu/), studies how to make AI systems more controllable and understandable. He is working to engineer simple, noninvasive ways to keep systems on task. Jha and his collaborators have authored a paper titled “Drift No More: Spectral Locking for Long-Horizon Open-Weight Multi-agent AI,” detailing a system dubbed ATLAS, which stands for Algebraic Task-role Locking with Adaptive Spectral Memory. The acronym hints at the dense mathematics at the heart of the system. Jha and his co-authors, Vishal Pramanik, Maisha Maliha and Susmit Jha, devised a system that works inside the large language model, or LLM, as it generates responses. When the model begins to drift from its task, ATLAS steers the LLM back toward its assignment, its original responsibility and the latest shared information. “An AI agent can start going wrong before its response looks wrong,” Jha explained. “Inside the model, it may already be drifting from the task, confusing its role or relying on information that has been replaced. ATLAS is built to correct that drift before it reaches the next message, so other agents do not build on the mistake.” Existing strategies usually involve policing an AI agent’s answers after the fact. ATLAS doesn’t require retraining the model, which means it doesn’t expend significant additional computing resources or slow the model down. According to Jha, so-called task drift is especially possible when groups of AI agents coordinate with one another over long periods. “Say, initially you told the agent you’re a doctor, you are supposed to treat patients,” Jha said. “By day No. 7, the fact that it's a doctor and it should treat patients is now just one of the millions of things it has been told. We call this problem role decoherence. The agent was tasked to do something, but after some time it has been told so many other things, it loses track." AI teams are usually managed from the outside through prompts, retrieval systems and rules, Jha said. “ATLAS adds something closer to an internal compass,” he said. “In mathematical form, it continually reinforces three questions: What is the mission? What is this agent responsible for? And what is true right now? Keeping those three answers aligned is essential when a task lasts much longer than a single conversation.” It sounds complicated, but as Jha tells it, the system is akin to taking MRI snapshots of the AI’s “brain,” or neural network.
