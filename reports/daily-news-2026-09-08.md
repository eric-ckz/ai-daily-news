Logo, The Next Platform](/view-resources/dachser2/public/nextplatform/logo-white.svg)](https://www.nextplatform.com) NEXTPLATFORM AD ![](https://image.nextplatform.com/5249714.webp?imageId=5249714&width=960&height=548&format=jpg) # Dell Says AI Will Drive 75 Percent Of Datacenter Demand By 2030 Timothy Prickett Morgan [Timothy Prickett Morgan](https://www.nextplatform.com/author/tpmn) Co-Editor, Co-Founder, The Next Platform Published It may have taken many years and what we presume was a certain amount of patience as Michael Dell watched his custom server maker rivals working for the hyperscalers and clouds get the vast majority of GPU allocations, but AI is a driving force for the company that bears his name. And so is an inevitable server upgrade cycle that has been a long time coming – and that has been delayed in part by AI spending and then accelerated as companies try to free up power and space in their datacenters by modernizing their application and database server fleets to make way for power-hungry AI servers. NEXTPLATFORM AD This is the opposite of the perfect storm, which is compounding bad news. As far as I know, we don’t have a name for that converse, so I guess we can just call it a harmonic resurgence. And now that the partners of Nvidia and AMD can make more GPU accelerators than they could several years ago, Dell is getting a lot more GPU allocations, and I think a disproportionately larger part of AI system orders because it has its supply chain and factories in order and can actually line up iron to run against its swelling GenAI revenue backlog. In a conference call going over the company’s second quarter of fiscal 2027 financial results, Jeff Clarke, Dell’s chief operating officer and vice chairman, said that it took eight quarters to get 3,200 customers who had bought an “AI factory.” And the next 3,300 customers were closed in only three quarters, for a total of 6,500 unique organizations that have deployed AI systems with PowerEdge brands on them. A lot of those customers are Tier 2 clouds and service providers as well as neoclouds and sovereigns, but now large enterprises are also looking to Dell, their traditional server supplier, to not only upgrade their aging iron, but to supply AI clusters for inference and maybe some training as well. Dell’s server business is hitting on all cylinders, and Clarke explained that there were over 1.2 million servers in the Dell installed base that were at the PowerEdge 14G generation or older. The 14G machines were announced in 2017 and ramped in 2018. So this iron in on the order of eight years old or older. That is a long time to keep a server in the field, and the current 17G machines from two years ago and the forthcoming 18G machines due next month allow workloads to be consolidated massively. Clarke pegs it at anywhere from six to eight of these vintage servers collapsed to one 17G system, and anywhere from twelve to fourteen for one 18G machine. These are much more heavily configured 17G or 18G machines that customers are buying to do consolidations, and so they are inherently more expensive. Dell is also passing on increasing costs for DRAM, flash, CPUs, and other peripherals, which further boosts revenues for traditional servers.
