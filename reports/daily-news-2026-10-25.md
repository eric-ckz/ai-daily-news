Yet an important interoperability question remains: how does one agent find another when neither person owns a domain name or operates a public discovery service? NandaIndex introduces an open resolution layer for the agentic web. Its purpose is narrow: given a stable identifier, return a pointer to the appropriate discovery object or system. It does not host the agent, determine whether an agent is trustworthy, or replace the protocols used to communicate with it. # The missing participants in agent discovery Enterprise-oriented discovery usually begins with organizational control of internet infrastructure. A company may publish an AI Catalog at a well-known URL, configure DNS-based service discovery, or expose agents through a governed gateway. These patterns are appropriate when the organization owns a domain, runs an IT function, and maintains published endpoints. The situation is different for a neighborhood bakery, a tutoring business, a home repair provider, or an independent professional. Some do not have a domain at all. Others own a domain but use a hosted site, social media, or an ecommerce service without the ability or resources to publish agent discovery metadata. Their agent card may be hosted by one provider and their agent runtime by another. Personal agents face an even more basic constraint: people commonly have email addresses and account identities rather than individually controlled domains. # From personal assistants to reachable agents The emerging personal-agent ecosystem makes this problem concrete. Haven similarly provides a personal assistant accessible through calls, messages, and an application. These examples indicate a shift from agents used only inside a product to agents expected to coordinate with external services. Naming these products does not imply that they currently support NandaIndex, or that their providers have committed to its integration. The question is architectural: if an agent deployed on any of these systems needs to exchange messages or tasks with an independently operated agent, how can it locate that agent without relying on a bilateral integration? Consider a consumer asking a personal agent to place an order with a local bakery. The bakery might have an agent that understands inventory, pickup availability, and ordering, but no catalog server or enterprise gateway. The consumer may have an agent running with a separate provider and no personal DNS entry. The deployment of large [GPU clusters](/tag/gpu+clusters), such as NVIDIA’s 72-GPU Vera Rubin model, is increasing rack power densities from tens to hundreds of kilowatts, with some designs projected to exceed one megawatt per rack. These [AI workloads](/tag/ai+workloads) behave differently from conventional applications, causing rapid utilization fluctuations from approximately 30 per cent to full in milliseconds during training cycles. However, AI workloads rarely operate in a steady-state, spending more time at partial loads where efficiency often declines. Even a one- or two-percentage-point drop in efficiency can lead to substantial energy losses across hundreds or thousands of GPU racks. This inefficiency increases heat generation, cooling requirements, and operational costs. ### High-Voltage Distribution and Flat-Load Efficiency Traditional 48-54V DC busbar architectures struggle to scale with increasing rack power requirements. Lower voltages demand higher currents for the same power, leading to resistive losses, larger cabling needs, and increased material costs, such as 200 kilograms of copper for a 1MW rack. Data centre operators are now considering two strategies: adopting higher-voltage power distribution architectures, such as ±400V or [800V DC](/tag/800v+dc) models, and maintaining flatter efficiency profiles across the full operating range. Higher voltages reduce current, copper requirements, and resistive losses, supporting greater rack densities. Flat-load efficiency ensures consistently high performance across the fluctuating load ranges typical of AI workloads. This approach helps reduce energy losses, lower heat generation, and improve the predictability and scalability of power systems.
