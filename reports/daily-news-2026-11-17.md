4. **Unlocking Enterprise AI Success: Beyond Individual Agents to Trustworthy Decisions, ETCIO**
   - 摘要：- Artificial Intelligence - 4 min read # Why enterprise AI success takes more than deploying a few AI agents Organizations are increasingly adopting multi-agent AI systems, which can improve business outcomes but may weaken ethical standards. Leaders must establish governed AI operating models to address these challenges effectively. It is essential for enterprises to ensure their AI agents make trustworthy decisions aligned with organizational objectives. Upcoming enterprises will need to emphasize accountability and governance as AI becomes more autonomous. When companies think about AI risk today, most of the focus remains at the model level: Can the LLM hallucinate? Will it follow policies? Does our information remain confidential? These are all important questions that we need to ask. As AI becomes more prevalent in our lives, leaders need to expand their view of risks. As organisations move from singular AI agents to multiple copilots’ networks of specialized AI agents, the challenge is no longer understanding singular agents but how groups of AI agents behave when they work together. This bifurcation can become wider with AI agents. In a supply chain scenario, one agent might overlook demand, another optimize pricing, another identify cost savings and another create an execution plan. While these specializations can make work faster, it doesn’t automatically mean alignment. An agent improving margins might overlook customer trust, while one reducing costs may miss service impacts. An agent reviewing the final output may check whether the task was completed without considering if the recommendation aligns with the organisation's objectives. **The next management challenge of enterprise AI** Smart agents alone will not be enough to manage these risks. Enterprises will need a governed AI operating model. This is where a governed AI operating model becomes important, one designed to make enterprise planning and execution more agile, adaptive and autonomous without losing sight of accountability. But autonomy without governance does not amount to transformation. It can simply mean that risk scales alongside automation. If AI agents were to optimize individual tasks without context of the enterprise data, they would produce much faster and more sophisticated recommendations that should not be trusted without verification. Leadership therefore needs to move beyond asking, "How many AI agents do we have?" and start asking, "Can we trust the decisions our AI organisation is making?" **The path forward requires a different architecture** Prompts by themselves are not enough to give AI agents the guidance they require. Business standards, operating regulations, financial considerations, legal needs, customer promises, and escalation levels are all parts of a shared system that organisations require to function. This enables AI agents to operate as an interconnected, cohesive decision-making system. Clear decision-making rights are also needed for these enterprises. Leaders need to specify which decisions should be automated, which require human interaction and which will not need AI automation. Additionally, the workflow itself also requires governance. Controls must function at the same pace as decision-making becomes increasingly independent. Leaders should be able to track the information being used, the assumptions they make, the risks they identify and the logic behind their decisions. Organisations should evaluate if their decision met the requirements for speed and accuracy, adhered to company policies, identified risks and produced insights for further decisions. This helps companies move from unregulated adoption to well-established and disciplined AI management by establishing a continuous learning loop. Beyond isolated copilots, the next phase of enterprise AI will involve networks of specialised agents in planning, finance, supply chains, procurement, services, and operations.
