Most publishers cannot afford to run it. Ivan Massow · 8 min read [View illustration full size ↗](../../assets/img/manual/manual-2026-10-18-agentic-journalism-v1.png) Cartoon · AI-generated Publishers are turning to AI agents to find original stories their readers cannot get anywhere else. The ones trying it are finding that the searching, reading and checking behind each story runs up bills that drain budgets within weeks. The answer is not a cheaper model. It is the oldest arrangement in news. Picture the editor of a specialist title who has just watched an AI agent at work. It has found, in a Singapore procurement register, a bridge contract that matters to engineers reading New Civil Engineer in Bristol. It has spotted a development on a new HIV medicine that an LGBT publication in Hove would lead on. It has pulled a planning dispute out of a council portal that nobody else has noticed. The editor wants it switched on tomorrow. That is how agentic journalism is arriving in newsrooms. And the reason publishers want it is not the one usually given. ## This is about better stories, not cheaper ones Advertisement · Noah The familiar AI story in publishing is cost-cutting: fewer people producing the same articles. That is not what is pulling serious publishers towards agents. The attraction is reach. An agent can watch thousands of records no newsroom could staff, and surface the stories that matter to a particular trade, community or town before anyone else writes them. For a specialist or local title, original material fitted to its own readers is the whole commercial case. AP saw this early. In September 2024 it announced a pilot with AppliedXL to find local news tips in federal regulatory material from more than 430 agencies, combining automated checks with human oversight, to help newsrooms extend their reporting.[A] The aim was discovery, not replacement. The trouble is what discovery costs. ## The meter runs long before the story is found The finished article is a poor guide to the work behind it. To find one worthwhile story, an agent has to locate and keep revisiting council portals, procurement registers, court lists, police updates, company filings and health authority notices. It has to retrieve documents, read them, spot what has changed and throw away the routine. Then every promising lead opens a fresh investigation. What happened before? Is this a tender or an award? Who benefits? What do the parties say? Every document read and every reasoning step consumes tokens, the metered units of text a model reads and writes. Search and data services charge on top. A dead end costs as much to explore as a scoop. So there are two bills: the breadth needed to notice a story, and the depth needed to stand it up. Both run whether or not anything publishable comes out the other end. Agents also spend faster than people expect. Anthropic's own measurements of its research system found agents typically used about four times the tokens of a chat interaction, and multi-agent systems about fifteen times, with duplicated searches and agents carrying on after they already had enough.[B] A newsroom that budgets an autonomous investigation like a handful of chatbot exchanges is budgeting for the wrong thing. ## Budgets are running dry mid-month Publishers are already hitting the wall. At a recent meeting at what Noah founder Ivan describes as "a tier-one international newspaper that we're fortunate enough to call a client", a consultant said their ChatGPT allowance was regularly exhausted around halfway through the month.
