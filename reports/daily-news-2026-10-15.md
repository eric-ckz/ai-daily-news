[By Jake Cole](/author/jake_coe)·5 min read·Updated October 14, 2026 This article has been reviewed by the Quartz editorial team. The content may be licensed for reuse. [Add QZ to Google](https://www.google.com/preferences/source?q=qz.com) AI developers are exploring ways to establish where models originated, but research suggests that existing fingerprinting techniques may be easier to remove than their creators expect. As artificial intelligence models become easier to copy, modify, and redistribute, proving where they came from is becoming a more complicated problem. Model weights can circulate without their original documentation, while fine-tuning and distillation can produce systems that retain much of a model’s functionality without preserving an obvious record of its source. The [Sentient Foundation’s Fingerprinting Leaderboard](https://fingerprint.sentient.foundation/) aims to address that challenge by providing an open benchmark for comparing techniques designed to identify AI models. The platform is intended to evaluate how well fingerprints survive attempts to remove them and how much they affect model performance. The initiative follows research by Sentient-affiliated researchers and collaborators showing that fingerprinting techniques can remain vulnerable even when they perform well under conventional testing conditions. In the paper, [“Are Robust LLM Fingerprints Adversarially Robust?”](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.26598), researchers developed adaptive attacks against 10 proposed fingerprinting schemes, finding that all could be bypassed while maintaining high model utility. The findings highlight a broader challenge for AI developers: Embedding a fingerprint is one thing, but ensuring that it remains detectable when someone actively tries to remove it is another. AI model fingerprinting attempts to make a model identifiable through signals embedded in, or detectable from, its behavior. Rather than relying exclusively on metadata, ownership records or training logs, these methods seek evidence that can be tested against a suspected derivative model. Sentient’s approach embeds hidden behavioral signatures during training. A model owner can subsequently submit specially designed challenge prompts to a suspected derivative and analyze its responses for traces of the original fingerprint. The approach could provide an additional way to investigate whether a model originated from another developer’s work. Its effectiveness, however, depends on whether the identifying signals survive subsequent training and modification. “AI models are becoming some of the most valuable intellectual property in the world, yet once a model is copied or distilled, proving ownership becomes incredibly difficult,” said Sachi Kamiya, director of venture and growth at Sentient Foundation. “Fingerprinting puts the proof inside the model,” Kamiya said. “Think of it as the AI equivalent of the hidden marks Renaissance painters used to establish authorship.” The underlying issue extends beyond attribution. Developers releasing models for public use may want to encourage experimentation without losing the ability to demonstrate that their work has been reused. More reliable provenance techniques could help support that goal, although a fingerprint alone would not necessarily establish legal ownership or prove that copying was unauthorized. Research presented at NeurIPS 2025 demonstrated that fingerprinting can operate at considerable scale.
