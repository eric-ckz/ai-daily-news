The problem is rarely the AI itself, but a lack of system-wide coherence – underscoring the need for clear cross-functional ownership and practical operational safeguards before scaling. When corporate AI agent programmes stall, the pattern is remarkably consistent. A system passes its pilot and risk review, only to unravel once a dozen or more autonomous agents are running at the same time. Approval rates drift and nobody can explain why; human review queues fill up with bizarre cases no one anticipated. The underlying AI is not broken. What is missing is something more specific than high-level AI governance: no one is responsible for the coherence of the whole system. Coherence means that hundreds of autonomous decisions, made across several workflows in a single day, are consistent with one another, with the policy in force at that moment, and with the data each agent can actually see. Mature industries have spent decades building shared standards to keep their records consistent – banks know the exact moment a payment is final, and supply chains rely on a single accurate count of what is in stock. Agentic AI has no equivalent discipline yet. The cause is structural. Engineering owns latency, the business owns return, compliance owns policy text and operations owns incidents.    - 摘要：Computer scientists have introduced FalsifyAgent, a verification framework designed to audit conclusions produced by large language model scientific agents by subjecting them to backward abductive reconstruction and adversarial co-evolution. Reported in the Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences by Hui Xiao, Hongwei Wang, and Meng Wang, the system addresses structural vulnerabilities in standard confirmatory verification methods that solely seek positive evidence matches for forward reasoning chains. Conventional checking paradigms often fail to detect omitted logical dependencies, ignore mutually exclusive alternative hypotheses supported by the same evidence, and remain susceptible to criterion-aware generation where models produce formally verifiable yet substantively inaccurate statements. To counteract these limitations, the authors recast trustworthiness from a static binary pass or fail check into procedural survival against progressively stronger adversarial challenges, establishing an evaluation framework grounded in the philosophy of scientific falsification. The theoretical architecture of FalsifyAgent centers on the Adversarially Robust Credibility Score, which quantifies credibility as the continuous area under a conclusion’s survival curve across iterative generations of falsification testing. Under uniform positive conditional bounds on attack coverage and correct adjudication, the researchers proved a conditional decay bound showing that the expected score for an objectively false assertion diminishes toward zero as testing generations increase. Rather than assuming unattacked reasoning chains are dependable, the framework enforces three axiomatic principles: conclusions must explicitly define testable operational failure conditions to be admitted, credibility can only be accumulated by surviving structured challenges, and every adversarial challenge must carry verifiable evidence rather than speculative objections. In the case of Europe, the question goes beyond technology and is about access to the grid, investment decisions, and a capacity to realize the development opportunity. ## The next generation of AI infrastructure is taking shape Until now, a megawatt of power was a considerable amount in an entire data hall, but NVIDIA is now developing a system whereby a single rack can approach that level of consumption. The consequences for the mechanical and electrical systems are considerable, as well as for the economics of large-scale artificial intelligence. May 2025 saw the presentation of NVIDIA's roadmap for 800VDC, a significant deviation from the conventional data center electrical architecture. Eighteen months later, the transition is moving away from mere paper engineering. The components required are being developed, the reference architectures are becoming increasingly precise, and the first reported commercial deployments are emerging. The pace at which the market is changing has been illustrated by two recent developments. In March NVIDIA launched its DSX AI factory design, then in October it launched the expanded DSX platform and the DSX POD reference architecture. And then on 2 November, China's Envision announced what it described as the first commercial implementation of an integrated 800V power system in a large-scale AI data center. These developments, as individuals, are all technologically important. However, they are pointing to a much deeper change in the way AI infrastructure is going to be designed, built and operated. For years now, data centers have been built with one basic assumption: they provide a reliable power supply, a high-quality building, and enough computing equipment to deliver what the client has paid for. The coming generation of AI-based platforms are putting this assumption to the test. Everything, the computing equipment, power distribution, cooling, power storage, and even interfacing with the local power grid, has become increasingly integrated. As one who has spent much of my career at the intersection of technology, strategy, and data center design, I can tell you that this transition needs a lot more attention from both industry and investors. It is not only a question of increasing the power to the racks, which grow more and more demanding, but of reconsidering the conversion of power into useful work, the financing of the infrastructure, and the execution of projects in markets where the availability of energy is one of the most important restrictions on growth. ## Why 800 VDC is becoming an architectural necessity The rationale behind 800 VDC begins with a relatively simple engineering challenge: AI computing systems are consuming substantially more electricity within increasingly concentrated physical footprints. Traditional enterprise racks commonly operated at between 5 and 10 kW. High-density AI systems now exceed 100 kW, with future architectures targeting several hundred kilowatts per rack, and NVIDIA's Kyber roadmap extends that trajectory toward rack power requirements approaching 1 MW. When you get to that density, you are running up against the limitations of conventional distribution. Electric power is the product of voltage and current, and when you're dealing with relatively low voltage, you need a higher current. That means you need bigger wires, and more losses, and a greater demand on the wiring and electrical system. NVIDIA has highlighted the limitations of extending today's 54 VDC rack distribution architecture to megawatt-scale systems.
